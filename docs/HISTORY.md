# History notes — the facts behind the game data

This file only has what the game uses. Numbers are approximate. Where historians
disagree, the range is given. Game values derived from them are tagged [balanced] in RULES.md.

## Scale
- **1 unit ≈ 5 infantry divisions.** 1 step ≈ 1¼ divisions.
- US divisions were about twice the size of Allied ones, so US units have 5 steps.
- 1 turn = 1 month. December 1914 to November 1918 is 48 turns.

- **Russia's exit** (Brest-Litovsk, March 1918) freed nearly 50 German divisions for the
  West. Germany still left about 1 million soldiers in the East.
  → In the game, +8 units arrive Jan–Mar 1918.
- **US build-up:** war declared April 1917. About 194,000 men had sailed by the end of
  1917, about 1 million were in France by July 1918, and 2.09 million had gone overseas by
  the armistice. At the peak, about 10,000 crossed every day in mid-1918.
  → In the game, US units trickle in from late 1917 and flood in during mid-1918.

## The map (north to south)
- **Lanes 1–2 (Yser, Ypres):**
  - Belgium held the Yser coast.
  - Ypres was a salient fought over every year: First Ypres 1914, gas 1915,
    Passchendaele 1917.
  - The **Channel Ports** were the BEF's supply line, which is why GBR supply traces
    there in the game.
- **Lanes 3–5 (Artois, Arras):** Loos (1915), Vimy and Arras (1917). Lille and Cambrai
  were German-held rail towns.
- **Lane 6 (Somme):**
  - **Amiens** was the key rail junction behind the boundary between the British and
    French armies, and a target of the 1918 German offensive.
  - The 8 August 1918 attack there was the German army's "Black Day".
- **Lane 7 (Noyon/Oise):** in 1914 the Germans came within about 70 km of **Paris**.
- **Lane 8 (Aisne):** the Chemin des Dames ridge. The failed Nivelle Offensive here in
  April 1917 led to the French mutinies.
- **Lanes 5–8, March 1917:** Germany withdrew to the **Hindenburg Line**, built 3–48 km
  behind the old front. It shortened the line by about 50 km and freed roughly 10–13
  divisions.
- **Lane 10 (Argonne):** behind it ran the **Sedan–Mézières lateral railway**, the spine of
  German logistics and the objective of the 1918 Meuse-Argonne offensive.
- **Lane 11 (Verdun):**
  - A symbolic fortress, attacked from February to December 1916 (about 400,000 French
    and 350,000 German casualties).
  - It was supplied up the "Voie Sacrée" road from Bar-le-Duc.
  - The St-Mihiel salient was cut off by the Americans in September 1918.
- **Lane 12 (Lorraine/Vosges):** mountains and forest, and mostly quiet after 1914.

## Terrain → map effects (RULES.md §3)
- **Germans held the high ground.** After the Marne the Germans fell back to the Aisne
  and chose where to dig in. That gave them the ridges and forced the British and French
  to dig in low, wet ground.
  → Ridges are on the German side of the line.
- **Allied attacks went uphill.** The big battles in Champagne, on the Somme and at Arras
  were Allied attacks uphill against defences on drier, observed ground.
- **Flanders:** flat and wet, with a high water table, so trenches flooded and the mud was
  terrible. Shelling made it worse.
  → In the game, mud and a trench cap of 2.
- **Chalk** (Somme, Artois, Champagne) took deep dugouts: German dugouts in the Somme
  chalk had electric light and floorboards.
  → In the game, chalk reaches trench 3 early.
- **The Yser:** the Belgians flooded the Yser plain in October 1914, which ended the
  German advance on the coast.
  → In the game, the flooded box.
- **Argonne:** dense forest; the Germans entered it in September 1914 to link Champagne
  to Vauquois near Verdun.
  → In the game, forest.
- **Open rolling ground** at Cambrai (1917) and Amiens (1918) is where massed tanks did best.

## National traits (RULES.md §4)
- **German heavy howitzers:**
  - Germany had far more heavy field howitzers than its enemies in 1914–15, and they were
    better.
  - France's 1914 heavy artillery was weaker because the army relied on the 75 mm gun.
  - France formally admitted it needed heavier guns in October 1914.
- **The French 75:** it fired further, faster and more accurately than the German 77 mm.
  France had about 4,000 in 1914 and 17,000 by the end of the war.
- **French production surge:** 75 mm shell output went from 13,000 a day in August 1914
  to 180,000 a day in 1918. France built a heavy artillery arm of about 6,000 guns, and
  all of the US field artillery was French-made.
- **French tanks:**
  - In April 1917 France had about four times as many tanks available as Britain.
  - More than 3,000 Renault FTs were built, most of them in 1918, at up to about 200 a
    month from March 1918.
- **Kitchener's New Armies** weren't ready for battle until mid-1916, and the Somme was
  their first great test.
  → In the game, Green until Jan 1917.
- **Dominion shock troops:** the Canadian and Australian Corps were the BEF's spearheads,
  with a reputation for success. The Canadians took Vimy Ridge in April 1917 in a little
  over three hours.
  → In the game, 2 Elite units from Apr 1917.
- **US Army:** high morale, but early doctrine and inexperience cost it.
  → In the game, Green for 3 turns.

## Commanders (RULES.md §15)
- **Joseph Joffre** (French commander-in-chief 1914–Dec 1916):
  - The victor of the Marne.
  - Believed in constant offensives: Artois and Champagne in 1915.
  - Lost the confidence of the government after Verdun, and was moved aside and made a
    Marshal in December 1916.
- **Robert Nivelle** (Dec 1916 – May 1917):
  - Rose through successful counter-attacks at Verdun.
  - Promised a quick breakthrough on the Chemin des Dames. Its failure in April 1917 set
    off the mutinies, and Pétain replaced him.
- **Philippe Pétain:**
  - The defender of Verdun: "Courage! On les aura!", 10 April 1916.
  - Became chief of the General Staff on 29 April 1917, then commander-in-chief.
  - Restored the army with leave, better food and limited attacks.
- **Ferdinand Foch:** chosen in spring 1918 to coordinate all the Allied armies, at
  Clemenceau's urging.
- **Sir John French** (BEF 1914–Dec 1915): commanded the regular "Old Contemptibles". He
  had a difficult relationship with Joffre and Kitchener, and was replaced after Loos.
- **Sir Douglas Haig** (Dec 1915 – 1918):
  - Led the Somme, Passchendaele and the Hundred Days.
  - Lloyd George distrusted him and held reserves back in Britain over the winter of
    1917–18.
  - Issued the "Backs to the Wall" order on 11 April 1918.
- **Erich von Falkenhayn** (Chief of the German General Staff to Aug 1916): planned
  Verdun to wear down the French army. Replaced after Romania entered the war.
- **Hindenburg & Ludendorff** (Third Supreme Command, Aug 1916 – Oct 1918):
  - Brought in total mobilisation (the Hindenburg Programme), the Hindenburg Line and the
    1918 Spring Offensives.
  - Ludendorff called 8 August 1918 the "black day of the German Army".
- **John J. Pershing** (AEF):
  - Insisted that the Americans fight as an independent army rather than being fed into
    Allied units.
  - The words "Lafayette, we are here" were his aide Colonel Stanton's, on 4 July 1917.

## Why defending was stronger
- **Trenches:**
  - Barbed wire, machine guns and deep dugouts.
  - In December 1916 the Germans adopted **defence in depth**: an outpost zone, a battle
    zone 1,400–2,300 m deep, and counter-attack divisions.
  → In the game, trench levels rise over time, and "dug in" defenders don't retreat.
- **Rail:** defenders could shift reserves along their own railways faster than attackers
  could advance on foot. At the Marne, France moved a corps across the front in 5–6 days.
  → In the game, rail moves.
- **Breakthroughs stalled** once attackers outran their artillery and supply, even
  in 1918.
  → In the game, supply attrition.

## Big battles (rough casualties, all sides)
| Battle | Dates | Casualties |
|---|---|---|
| Marne | Sep 1914 | about 300,000 (estimates vary a lot) |
| First Ypres | Oct–Nov 1914 | BEF ~55,000, French 50,000–86,000, German 80,000–134,000 |
| Verdun | Feb–Dec 1916 | ~750,000 |
| Somme | Jul–Nov 1916 | over 1 million, for about 7 miles of ground. The British lost 57,000 on the first day. |
| Nivelle / Chemin des Dames | Apr–May 1917 | French 118,000–187,000 |
| Passchendaele | Jul–Nov 1917 | ~275,000 Allied, ~220,000 German (Britannica; estimates differ) |
| German Spring Offensives | Mar–Jul 1918 | ~863,000 German. Many were irreplaceable stormtroopers. |
| Hundred Days | Aug–Nov 1918 | ~1.07M Allied, 1.17M+ German, including 386,000 prisoners |

**Western Front military dead:**
- France ~1.4M
- Germany ~1.6M
- Britain ~700,000
- USA ~117,000 (about half from disease)
- Belgium ~80,000

## Technology timeline (game events)
- **Gas:** first lethal use in the West, with chlorine at Ypres in April 1915.
- **Shell crisis:** Britain was short of artillery shells in 1915.
  → Low GBR munitions in 1915.
- **Creeping barrage:**
  - First tried at scale on the Somme on 1 July 1916, where it failed.
  - Standard by September 1916; perfected at Vimy and Arras in April 1917.
- **Tanks:**
  - First used at Flers-Courcelette on 15 September 1916, where only 36 reached the
    start line.
  - First used in mass at Cambrai in November 1917; over 500 at Amiens in August 1918.
  - Germany built only about 20 of its own (A7V).
- **Stormtrooper / infiltration tactics:** used at scale from 21 March 1918.
- **Combined arms:** artillery, tanks, aircraft and infantry working together in the
  Hundred Days (Aug–Nov 1918).

## Home front and national will
- **Blockade:**
  - German imports fell about 55% by 1915, and rationing began in January 1915.
  - The "Turnip Winter" of 1916–17 was the worst.
  - Estimates of German civilian deaths from hunger range from about 300,000 to
    760,000; about 500,000 is a common figure.
  → In the game, a growing German Will loss from 1916.
- **U-boats:**
  - Unrestricted submarine warfare was first declared in February 1915 (the Lusitania was
    sunk in May 1915), then suspended.
  - It resumed in February 1917, aiming to knock Britain out within 5–6 months, and
    sinkings peaked in April 1917.
  - Convoys from May 1917 cut losses. The US declared war in April 1917.
  → In the game, the U-boat decision.
- **French mutinies, May–June 1917:**
  - Unrest spread through many divisions; sources say 21 to 54.
  - Pétain restored order with better conditions and by staying on the defensive for the
    rest of 1917.
  → In the game, no French attacks for 3 turns.
- **German collapse, autumn 1918:**
  - Germany's allies fell: Bulgaria in September, then the Ottomans and Austria-Hungary.
  - At home came hunger, influenza, the naval mutiny of late October and then revolution.
  - Ludendorff told the leadership on 29 September that the front could not hold.
  → In the game, Germany loses when its Will hits 0.
- **Armistice, 11 November 1918:**
  - Germany had to leave France, Belgium and Alsace-Lorraine within 15 days.
  - The Allies occupied the Rhineland.
  - Germany handed over 5,000 guns, 25,000 machine guns, 1,700 aircraft and its U-boats.

## Drawing the map
How the campaign map (`img/map-west.svg`) was made, and how accurate each part is.
- **Coast, countries, rivers:**
  - From **Natural Earth** (public domain, naturalearthdata.com): 10m countries and land
    via the `world-atlas` package, and 10m rivers plus the European supplement.
  - Today's Franco-German border on the Rhine is left out, because in 1914 the border was
    the 1871 one.
- **Contour lines:**
  - From the **Terrarium elevation tiles** on AWS Open Data, made by Mapzen from SRTM,
    ETOPO1, EU-DEM and other public sources. They ask for attribution, which this line
    gives.
  - About 200 m per pixel, so small hills read low (Mont Kemmel comes out at 87 m; it is
    really 156 m).
- **Hand-traced** in `tools/map-extra.json`, by Claude, from general reference
  knowledge and modern maps of the same places. Please check them against a period map
  before relying on them for rules.
  - **The front line** is the line as it stood for most of 1915–1917, about ±2 km:
    Nieuport, the Yser, the Ypres salient, Armentières, La Bassée, Vimy, Arras, Albert,
    Roye, Noyon, Soissons, the Chemin des Dames, Reims, Champagne, the Argonne, Verdun,
    St-Mihiel, Pont-à-Mousson, the Vosges, and Pfetterhouse on the Swiss border.
    - A good public-domain check is the US Military Academy (West Point) atlas maps of
      the Western Front.
  - **The 1871–1918 border** (Treaty of Frankfurt) runs from Luxembourg past Thionville
    and Metz on the German side, then down the Vosges crest to Switzerland. About ±3 km.
  - **The Yser, Oise and Aisne:** traced through their main towns, about ±2 km.
  - **Ridges:** crest lines of the high ground fought over (Messines–Passchendaele,
    Aubers, Lorette, Vimy, Thiepval–Pozières, the Chemin des Dames, the Montagne de
    Reims, the Monts de Champagne, the Argonne, Mort-Homme and Hill 304, the Heights of
    the Meuse, the Vosges).
  - **Railways:**
    - The main lines of 1914, traced station to station. The stations are right; the
      track between them is straight-ish and approximate (±3 km).
    - Natural Earth's railways were not used, because they are today's network.
    - The German lateral line (Lille–Valenciennes–Hirson–Mézières–Sedan–Metz) was the
      spine of German supply.
  - **Roads:** routes that mattered, including the Voie Sacrée (Bar-le-Duc–Verdun), the
    Menin Road, the Albert–Bapaume road and the Chemin des Dames.
  - **Villages and landmarks:** positions from modern gazetteers, about ±1 km.
    - Heights: Mort-Homme 295 m, Hill 304, the Butte de Vauquois about 290 m.
    - Hill 60 was a 60 m spoil heap near Zillebeke.
- **Trench colours** follow the British trench maps of 1917, which printed German
  trenches in red and Allied trenches in blue.
- **Battle scars and pencil notes** are drawn from the game's own battles, not real
  history. `js/ui/demo.js` holds a sample war roughly following the real one (Artois and
  Champagne 1915, Verdun and the Somme 1916, Arras, the Chemin des Dames and
  Passchendaele 1917).
